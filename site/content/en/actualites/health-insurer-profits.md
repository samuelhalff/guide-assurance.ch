Swiss health insurers cannot make a profit on basic insurance. The law reserves the money it brings in for social health insurance, and every surplus goes into reserves. Their profits come from supplementary insurance, which is governed by private law. FINMA puts the 2025 profit on supplementary business at CHF 869 million, while the basic insurance surplus of almost CHF 569 million reported by the Federal Office of Public Health (FOPH) went into reserves in full.

## What does the law say about profit in basic insurance?

The Health Insurance Supervision Act (LSAMal) sets the rule in two places. It defines health insurance funds as legal entities with no profit motive (art. 2 para. 1 LSAMal). It then requires every insurer licensed for compulsory health insurance, private insurance companies included, to use the resources it draws from that insurance only for social health insurance purposes (art. 5 let. f LSAMal).

In its reserves fact sheet of 29 September 2026, the FOPH sums up the rule: whatever the level of reserves, premium money may only be used to cover the costs of compulsory health insurance. Basic insurance assets must also be held separately (art. 15 LSAMal).

Basic insurance premiums need FOPH approval (art. 16 LSAMal). The FOPH refuses a tariff if the premiums fail to cover costs, exceed them inappropriately or lead to excessive reserves.

## What happens to basic insurance surpluses?

They go into reserves, which let an insurer absorb a bad year without a sudden jump in premiums (art. 14 LSAMal). According to the accounts the FOPH published on 2 July 2026, insurers recorded a total surplus of almost CHF 569 million in 2025. Insurance operations contributed CHF 58 million and investments CHF 511 million. All of it went into reserves, which stood at CHF 8.6 billion at the start of the year.

In 2024 the picture was reversed. Insurance operations lost CHF 404 million, while investments earned CHF 801 million. The overall result, close to CHF 400 million, also went into reserves, which stood at CHF 7.8 billion at the start of 2025.

The FOPH notes that investment income accounts for most of the growth in reserves over the past ten years, and that these gains are added to reserves in full. An insurer whose reserves exceed the legal minimum may, under certain conditions, reduce them by setting lower premiums or paying part of them back to policyholders.

The FOPH also reports that administrative costs in basic insurance reached CHF 1.7 billion in 2025, or CHF 191 per insured person and 4.3% of premiums.

## So where do health insurers' profits come from?

They come from supplementary insurance, which covers things such as private or semi-private hospital wards, dental care, complementary medicine and glasses. These policies fall under the Insurance Contract Act, and health insurance funds may offer them alongside basic insurance (art. 2 para. 2 LSAMal).

FINMA's insurance market report, published on 20 August 2026, gives the 2025 figures for health insurers selling this supplementary cover.

| Indicator | 2025 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|
| Gross premiums | CHF 11.4 billion | CHF 11.2 billion |
| Underwriting result | CHF 467 million | CHF 186 million |
| Investment profit | CHF 606 million | CHF 713 million |
| Annual profit | CHF 869 million | CHF 744 million |

The profit comes from the insurance business itself, when premiums exceed claims paid and costs, and from investment returns. In 2025 the underwriting result more than doubled compared with 2024, while investment profit fell. FINMA notes that claims payments went down in 2025 even though the supplementary portfolio grew by 0.5%.

These figures cover each health insurer as a whole, including any other lines of business it runs alongside health insurance. The market includes 16 supplementary health insurers based in Switzerland, two branches of foreign insurers and nine health insurance funds that also sell supplementary cover.

## Who supervises supplementary insurance premiums?

FINMA reviews supplementary insurance tariffs and approves them if the assumptions are plausible, the tariffs are not abusively high and they do not threaten the company's solvency. Its fact sheet states that tariffs may include a profit within that framework. In its 2025 report, FINMA says it only allows premium increases that rest on actuarial grounds and that it obtained premium cuts in several cases.

The two supervisors look at different things. The FOPH makes sure basic premiums cover costs and no more. FINMA checks that supplementary insurers stay solvent and that their tariffs are not abusive.

## What does this mean for policyholders?

In basic insurance, a higher premium does not fund a profit, and the benefits are the same with every insurer. Differences in premiums reflect costs, reserves and the model chosen. Comparing the approved premiums in your region is therefore still worthwhile, and you can switch insurer every year before the end of November.

In supplementary insurance, the insurer may ask health questions and turn an applicant down. If you cancel a supplementary policy before another insurer has accepted you in writing, you may end up without cover.
